New York State is home to some of America’s poorest cities, where many residents rely on federal assistance programs for their livelihood. In Onondaga County, where Syracuse holds the highest child poverty rate in the nation, programs like the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program are crucial to many families struggling with food insecurity.

The recent government shutdown brought attention to the importance of these programs as many of them lost their funding. [insert food pantry quote]

In July, the Trump administration signed the One Big Beautiful Act, bringing significant changes to federal programs like Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program formerly known as Food Stamps. The bill creates stricter eligibility requirements for applicants, making it less accessible to the 2.6 million New Yorkers living under the poverty line. Additionally, for the first time in history, the OBBA requires states to pay 75% of administrative costs instead of the previous 50%.

Altogether, these changes will result in thousands of New York families losing access to a program that is a crucial lifeline to many.

In Onondaga county, which has the state’s 14th highest rate of SNAP participants, the program’s funding has been on the decline for years. In the last decade, the state budget allotted for the program lost nearly one million when adjusted for inflation.

Additionally, the amount of people on SNAP benefits has also decreased despite little change in the number of people below the poverty line. [insert info from interview]

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention announced last month that people with shots could go maskless. The result is that mask-wearing is not normalized and disease could potentially spread more easily. Bringing back masks, Davis said, will coordinate the same practices for everyone, giving vaccinated people a boost and unvaccinated people life-saving protection.

The county reported 1,315 new cases as of Wednesday — the fifth day in a row with more than 1,000 new coronavirus infections, according to the Associated Press. Adults ages 18 to 29 are seeing the highest and fastest-rising case rates, in addition to middle-aged people, teens, and young children.
